Pilots have.

Today’s students can learn from anywhere and in their own way. Flight training still moves with paper, weather and memory. We provide a pilot-centric platform that supports an aviator’s personal journey, with certification and ratings as milestones and airmanship as the true goal.

    Friction and fragmentation

    Most students who start flight training never earn a certificate; dropout runs as high as 80%. Cost is a factor, but the main reasons are the quality of instruction and customer service.

    Flight training is organized around FAA certification, so pilots learn rote tasks while judgment and airmanship get little attention. Knowledge, skill building and in-airplane training are rarely integrated. Costs climb as lessons are repeated and the airplane becomes the classroom.

    Providers feel it too. Every student who quits is lost revenue. Outdated software tracks resources, not student progress, and weather and cancellations leave airplanes and instructors idle, even when other ways to learn exist. When an instructor leaves or a student changes schools, the records stay behind.

    Source: AOPA, The Flight Training Experience (2010).

    Integration and continuity

    One platform for flight training with three parts, learning, resource management and environmental factors, all built on our Learn-Do-Fly Training Standard and a Knowledge Library where every answer cites its source.

    Learning. Every training encounter follows the Learn-Do-Fly framework: knowledge acquisition, skill building with the sim or visualization, then performing in the airplane. The pilot is in command of their own learning: their record remains with them, and each session builds on the last. The Knowledge Library breaks expert knowledge, regulations and each school’s own material into small, sourced pieces we call atoms, delivered as an audio briefing, a visual, a sim exercise or a kneeboard card.

    Resource management. Aircraft, instructors, simulators, scheduling and billing are connected as one smart system, so instructors and administrators spend more time on each learner.

    Environmental factors. Weather, TFRs and NOTAMs are presented ahead of each session, so training opportunities aren’t lost to surprises. Decisions stay with a human.

    We all benefit from well-trained pilots

    Most pilots, including airline captains, start at a local flight school. At some point, all of us depend on the quality of the training that pilots receive.

    Worldwide, airlines will need about 674,000 new pilots over the next 20 years, and every one of them starts with flight training. The same need exists abroad; Australia is our first international market.

    Instructing counts toward airline flight time only in the airplane, so students get rushed into the airplane, which is a terrible classroom. We break that cycle by keeping the method and the record with the pilot, not the instructor.

    Source: Boeing, Pilot and Technician Outlook 2026–2045; 14 CFR 61.159.

    Four sources of friction and fragmentation
    Credentialism
    Training for the test, not for the transferable skills pilots need to adapt to new situations and manage risk
    Disintegration
    No seamless connection among knowledge acquisition, skill building and in-airplane training
    Inefficiency
    A predisposition for training in the airplane, when it should be where students apply what they’ve learned
    Regression
    Lost continuity between instructors and providers, a major factor in the dropout rate
